Can NC State Students Afford to Live Near Campus?

Sean Ryan, Correspondent · Oct. 8, 2026

For many NC State students, college marks a new chapter of independence. That independence often includes a new home. But that independence comes at a price. For students already balancing tuition, food and other expenses, housing can quickly become one of the highest costs of attending college.

NC State estimates that a student living on campus will spend $8,738 on housing during the 2026–27 academic year, compared to $9,808 for a student living off campus. The on-campus budget’s roughly $971 monthly equivalent is enough to cover the standard residence hall double shown below, but it falls short of the standard single.

The gap extends beyond the standard single. Once required fees are included, only three of the 10 housing categories published by University Housing fall within NC State’s $8,738 on-campus estimate. The finding is consistent with a 2023 report by Mary Haskett, professor emeritus of psychology at NC State, which found that the University’s housing estimate at the time could cover only four of 14 on-campus residence halls.

Students in their first year are generally required to live on campus, but a majority of NC State undergraduates, 62%, live off campus or commute, according to the University’s Common Data Set.

Thomas Barrie, an NC State professor of architecture who directs the University’s Affordable Housing and Sustainable Communities Initiative and teaches housing policy, said increased construction has helped cool the rental market.

“Rent has gone down. It’s dipped a little bit in downtown Raleigh, and it’s because we’ve actually built a lot of rental housing,” Barrie said. “But it’s still very expensive.”
While the Sept. 9 snapshot of listings from NC State’s Off-Campus Housing site found a lower mean advertised private-bedroom rent than either single or double residence hall monthly equivalent, the prices do not necessarily include the same costs.

Alden Hearn, housing navigator for HOST, a Wake County nonprofit serving college students experiencing homelessness or housing insecurity, said additional charges can substantially change what initially appears to be an affordable rent.

“So, if you’re a student and you’re thinking, okay, I can relocate to Wake County because I’m seeing places that only cost $700, with the aid that I get, I’ll be able to get a job, and I can use my aid to supplement that no problem,” Hearn said. “But then once you’re leased up, and you’ve got the guarantor waiver program and the amenities fee, potentially other fees, it often pushes towards $1,000 a month.”

Of the listings in the Sept. 9 scrape, 74 were identified as offering a bedroom intended for one occupant. Those listings had a mean advertised price of about $869 per month. Six listings, about 8% of the sample, explicitly advertised both all utilities and internet or Wi-Fi as included; their mean advertised rent was about $816 per month.

NC State housing officials said more than 70% of residence hall room inventory is double occupancy. The double room price shown above represents one student’s cost to share a bedroom with another student, with a monthly equivalent of about $916 per student. Residence hall rates include utilities, laundry, maintenance and student support, while internet is billed through a separate required ResNet fee.

Donna McGalliard, associate vice chancellor and associate dean for Student Life and Advocacy and executive director of University Housing, described double rooms as Housing’s “best kind of value.” She called the comparison between residence hall doubles and per-bedroom prices advertised off campus “apples and oranges,” saying University Housing compares on-campus apartments with off-campus apartments and considers amenities when setting rates.

For students, the practical question may still be which fruit offers more for the price.

Housing officials also emphasized that NC State students can sign an academic-year housing agreement rather than paying for housing through the summer. Summer housing is separate, and most residents who remain on campus during winter break pay an additional charge. McGalliard said that a shorter contract is one of the advantages of living on campus.

“For us, you arrive in August, you leave at the beginning of May, you’re done,” McGalliard said.

McGalliard said the complexity of operating campus housing makes it difficult to reduce its rates to a simple comparison.

“Trying to describe the comprehensive nature of what goes into these numbers. And this committee of the board of trustees already has that information because, I mean, again, we’re trying to not only adjust for utility costs that we have to pay for, we’re adjusting for the cost of the elevator contract and the grounds and housekeeping and landscaping and roof contracts. All of the things go into setting a rate to include the money to provide activities, the money for the staff that is taking care of the buildings, the ones who are living there, providing the behavioral response, et cetera,” McGalliard said.

When asked what specific numerical affordability target University Housing uses when setting rates, officials did not identify one during the interview. After the interview, University Housing was emailed the data and methodology used for review, but a follow-up email was not received. University Housing’s published projections show that eight of 10 housing options are expected to increase by more than the 3.1% national increase in the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ lodging-while-at-school index from August 2025 to August 2026.


As prices rise, students have to stretch each dollar further. North Carolina’s minimum wage has remained $7.25 an hour since July 2009. NC State’s Federal Work-Study program lists an allowable wage range of $7.25 to $15 an hour, with higher wages requiring a case-by-case exception. The university says work-study students generally should work no more than 20 hours per week, although they may hold another non-work-study job at the same time.

Professor Barrie said he often asks students who are struggling in their architecture studio courses how much they are working and whether they can afford to cut back.

“So how much are you working?” Barrie said he asks students. “And they’ll tell me, and I’ll say, ‘Do you need to work that much?’ And then often the answer is, ‘I do.’”

For those students, Barrie said, the answer is to take that financial reality “at face value” and work with it.

To compare advertised room rents with the resources available to a working student, this analysis uses the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development’s 30% housing cost-burden benchmark. HUD considers households spending more than 30% of their income on housing cost-burdened and says they may have difficulty affording other necessities such as food, clothing, transportation, and medical care.

The interactive comparison uses wages ranging from North Carolina’s $7.25 minimum wage to $15 an hour and allows readers to add NC State’s reported average need-based grant or need-based loan award. Since grants and loans are not the same as household income, the 30% line is a reference rather than a formal HUD cost-burden determination


At $15 an hour and NC State’s general work-study guideline of 20 hours per week, a student would have a gross monthly equivalent of about $1,300. The $869 mean advertised Main Campus private-bedroom rent would consume about 67% of those gross wages. Adding the average need-based loan lowers the illustrative share to about 53%, while adding the average need-based grant lowers it to about 34%. Combining both average awards in the hypothetical model brings the share to just over 30%. At 40 hours per week, twice the work-study guideline, the rent would consume about 33% of gross wages without financial aid.

Across the hours modeled, wages between North Carolina’s $7.25 minimum wage and NC State’s standard $15 work-study ceiling never bring the rent below HUD’s 30% housing cost-burden benchmark. Wages above $15 require a case-by-case exception. In this hypothetical model, only scenarios that include a need-based grant or loan can fall below that line.

However, the “Compare with need-based aid” tool treats the full average award as available for rent and does not first subtract tuition or other university charges. NC State says, “Financial aid must be used first to pay any outstanding tuition and fees, room rent and meal plan charges and textbook charges, if applicable, for each semester.” The amount actually available for off-campus rent may therefore be much smaller.

The consequences of those strained housing budgets show up in HOST’s work. The nonprofit began serving students in fall 2022 and became fully operational in early 2023. Since then, “we’ve served just over 100 students,” Hearn said.

For students already stretching their income to afford housing, Hearn said one unexpected expense can be enough to put their housing at risk.

“So, a lot of students are willing to take a chance on a housing situation where they think they can make ends meet, but a single setback, like if you get sick and you’re out of work for a couple of weeks, or if you lose your job, or if your car breaks down, just falling behind a few weeks or a month of income can basically torpedo the stability of that housing because there’s no other income coming in that’ll help you catch up,” Hearn said. “Once you’re evicted, then your options really narrow.”

Hearn called student homelessness “an invisible problem,” saying people often assume college students’ basic needs are stable.

“And also, you know, I think among college students, what homelessness looks like, you know, we worked with a lot of students that maybe because of sort of a popular narrative expect that they’re going to struggle with housing while they’re in college,” Hearn said. “And so they might fill in gaps by staying in a vehicle or especially couch surfing with friends if they don’t have family that they can return to during breaks.”

That pattern also appeared in a 2023 NC State student survey analyzed in a report authored by Mary Haskett, professor emerita of psychology and a founder of HOST. The study found that 14% of respondents, or 190 of 1,357 students, experienced homelessness during the previous year. Temporarily staying with a relative or friend, including couch surfing until the student found other housing, was the most commonly reported form of homelessness.

Students who had lived in a residence hall at some point during the year experienced a slightly higher rate of homelessness, 15.6%, than students who had not, 12.5%. The difference was not statistically significant, meaning the study does not establish that living in a residence hall increased the likelihood of homelessness. The researchers concluded that residence hall living did not protect students from experiencing homelessness.

Hearn said programs like HOST cannot meet the scale of the need without greater involvement from community and university leaders.

Haskett characterized NC State’s response to student housing insecurity as insufficient.

“Their response is one of what I refer to as charity,” Haskett said.

She pointed to housing-related scholarships as one example.

“Those are funded by donors. That’s not even university money,” Haskett said. “So the university is not making any investment in those scholarships.”

For Haskett, the consequences extend beyond where students live to whether they finish college.

“It’s not about just increasing enrollment,” Haskett said. “You need to increase graduation. Students can’t graduate if they’re dropping out because they can’t afford to live here.”
